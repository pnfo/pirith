The Great Discourse on the Foundations of Mindfulness

Thus have I heard.
At one time the Blessed One
was living among the Kurus,
at a market town of the Kurus named Kammāsadamma.
There the Blessed One
addressed the monks: ‘Monks.’
‘Venerable sir,’ those monks
replied to the Blessed One.
The Blessed One said this:

Monks, this is the direct path
for the purification of beings,
for overcoming sorrow and lamentation,
for the ending of pain and grief,
for attaining the true way,
for the realization of Nibbana,
namely, the four foundations of mindfulness.
What four?
Here, monks,
a monk dwells contemplating the body in the body,
ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful,
having put away covetousness and grief for the world;

he dwells contemplating feelings in feelings,
ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful,
having put away covetousness and grief for the world;

he dwells contemplating mind in mind,
ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful,
having put away covetousness and grief for the world;

he dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas,
ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful,
having put away covetousness and grief for the world.

The summary is finished.

And how, monks,
does a monk dwell contemplating the body in the body?
Here, monks, a monk,
gone to the forest or to the foot of a tree
or to an empty hut,
sits down cross-legged,
holds his body straight,
and sets up mindfulness before him.
Mindful, he breathes in;
mindful, he breathes out.
Breathing in long,
he knows ‘I breathe in long’;
breathing out long,
he knows ‘I breathe out long.’
Breathing in short,
he knows ‘I breathe in short’;
breathing out short,
he knows ‘I breathe out short.’
He trains: ‘I will breathe in experiencing the whole body’;
he trains: ‘I will breathe out experiencing the whole body.’
He trains: ‘I will breathe in calming the bodily formation’;
he trains: ‘I will breathe out calming the bodily formation.’

Just as, monks,
a skilled turner
or a turner’s apprentice,
making a long turn,
knows ‘I make a long turn,’
or making a short turn,
knows ‘I make a short turn,’
so too, monks,
a monk, breathing in long,
he knows ‘I breathe in long’;
breathing out long,
he knows ‘I breathe out long.’
Breathing in short,
he knows ‘I breathe in short’;
breathing out short,
he knows ‘I breathe out short.’
He trains: ‘I will breathe in experiencing the whole body’;
he trains: ‘I will breathe out experiencing the whole body.’
He trains: ‘I will breathe in calming the bodily formation’;
he trains: ‘I will breathe out calming the bodily formation.’

Thus he dwells contemplating the body in the body internally,
or he dwells contemplating the body in the body externally,
or he dwells contemplating the body in the body both internally and externally.

Or he dwells contemplating the nature of arising in the body,
or he dwells contemplating the nature of passing away in the body,
or he dwells contemplating the nature of both arising and passing away in the body.

Or else, that ‘there is a body’,
mindfulness is established in him,
just to the extent needed for knowledge and mindfulness.
He dwells independent,
and clings to nothing in the world.
In this way too, monks,
a monk dwells contemplating the body in the body.

The section on breathing is finished.

Again, monks,

In this body there are

Contemplation of Feelings

And how, monks,
a monk dwells contemplating feelings in feelings?
Here, monks, a monk,
when feeling a pleasant feeling
he knows, 'I feel a pleasant feeling',
or when feeling a painful feeling
he knows, 'I feel a painful feeling'.
Or when feeling a neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling
he knows, 'I feel a neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling'.

Or when feeling a worldly pleasant feeling
he knows, 'I feel a worldly pleasant feeling'.
Or when feeling an unworldly pleasant feeling
he knows, 'I feel an unworldly pleasant feeling'.
Or when feeling a worldly painful feeling
he knows, 'I feel a worldly painful feeling'.
Or when feeling an unworldly painful feeling
he knows, 'I feel an unworldly painful feeling'.
Or when feeling a worldly neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling
he knows, 'I feel a worldly neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling'.
Or when feeling an unworldly neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling
he knows, 'I feel an unworldly neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling'.

Thus he dwells contemplating feelings in feelings internally,
or he dwells contemplating feelings in feelings externally,
or he dwells contemplating feelings in feelings both internally and externally.
Or he dwells contemplating the nature of arising in feelings,
or he dwells contemplating the nature of passing away in feelings,
or he dwells contemplating the nature of both arising and passing away in feelings.

Or the mindfulness that 'there are feelings'
mindfulness is established in him,
just to the extent needed for knowledge and mindfulness.
He dwells independent,
and clings to nothing in the world.
In this way too, monks,
a monk dwells contemplating feelings in feelings.

The contemplation of feelings is finished.

Again, monks,
a monk dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas
in terms of the four noble truths.
And how is it, monks, that
a monk dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas
in terms of the four noble truths?

Here, monks, a monk,
understands as it really is: 'This is suffering';
he understands as it really is: 'This is the arising of suffering';
he understands as it really is: 'This is the cessation of suffering';
he understands as it really is: 'This is the way leading to the cessation of suffering.'

And what, monks, is the noble truth of suffering?
Birth is suffering,
ageing is suffering, death is suffering;
sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief and despair are suffering;
association with the disliked is suffering,
separation from the liked is suffering,
not getting what one wishes for is also suffering;
in short, the five aggregates of clinging are suffering.

And what, monks, is birth?
For the various beings,
in the various orders of beings,
their birth, coming to be, descent, production,
the appearance of the aggregates, the gaining of the sense bases:
this, monks, is called birth.

And what, monks, is ageing?
For the various beings,
in the various orders of beings,
their ageing, decay, broken teeth, grey hair, wrinkled skin,
the dwindling of life, the decline of the faculties:
this, monks, is called ageing.

And what, monks, is death?
For the various beings,
from the various orders of beings,
their passing away, removal, breaking up, disappearance,
dying, completion of their time,
the breaking up of the aggregates, the laying down of the body, the cutting off of the life faculty:
this, monks, is called death.

And what, monks, is sorrow?
Monks, for one afflicted by some misfortune or other,
touched by some painful thing or other,
the sorrow, sorrowing, sorrowfulness,
inner sorrow, deep inner sorrow:
this, monks, is called sorrow.

And what, monks, is lamentation?
Monks, for one afflicted by some misfortune or other,
touched by some painful thing or other,
the wailing, lamenting, act of wailing, act of lamenting,
the state of wailing and lamenting:
this, monks, is called lamentation.

And what, monks, is pain?
Monks, whatever bodily pain, bodily discomfort,
painful, unpleasant feeling born of bodily contact:
this, monks, is called pain.

And what, monks, is grief?
Monks, whatever mental pain, mental discomfort,
painful, unpleasant feeling born of mind contact:
this, monks, is called grief.

And what, monks, is despair?
Monks, for one afflicted by some misfortune or other,
touched by some painful thing or other,
the trouble, despair, state of being troubled, state of despair:
this, monks, is called despair.

And what, monks, is the suffering of association with the disliked?
Here, whoever has
unwanted, unlovely, disagreeable
sights, sounds, smells, tastes, touches and mind-objects,
or whoever has those who
wish him harm, ill, discomfort and no safety from bondage,
the meeting, coming together, encounter and mixing with them:
this, monks, is called the suffering of association with the disliked.

And what, monks, is the suffering of separation from the liked?
Here, whoever has
wanted, lovely, agreeable
sights, sounds, smells, tastes, touches and mind-objects,
or whoever has those who
wish him good, welfare, comfort and safety from bondage,
mother or father,
brother or sister,
elders or juniors,
friends, colleagues or blood relatives,
the not meeting, not coming together, not encountering and not mixing with them:
this, monks, is called the suffering of separation from the liked.

And what, monks, is 'not getting what one wishes for is also suffering'?
Monks, in beings subject to birth

if only birth would not come to us!'

This too is 'not getting what one wishes for is also suffering'.

And what, monks, are, in short, the five aggregates of clinging that are suffering?
They are: the form aggregate of clinging, the feeling aggregate of clinging,
the perception aggregate of clinging, the formations aggregate of clinging, the consciousness aggregate of clinging.
These, monks, are called, in short, the five aggregates of clinging that are suffering.
This, monks, is called the noble truth of suffering.

And what, monks, is the noble truth of the arising of suffering?
It is this craving that leads to renewed existence,
accompanied by delight and lust, delighting here and there,
namely: craving for sensual pleasures, craving for existence, craving for non-existence.

And what, monks,
is the noble truth of the way leading to the cessation of suffering?
It is just this Noble Eightfold Path,
namely:
right view, right intention, right speech, right action,
right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.

And what, monks, is right view?
It is, monks, the knowledge of suffering,
the knowledge of the arising of suffering,
the knowledge of the cessation of suffering,
and the knowledge of the way leading to the cessation of suffering.
This, monks, is called right view.

And what, monks, is right intention?
The intention of renunciation, the intention of non-ill-will, the intention of harmlessness.
This, monks, is called right intention.

And what, monks, is right speech?
Abstaining from false speech,
abstaining from divisive speech,
abstaining from harsh speech,
abstaining from idle chatter.
This, monks, is called right speech.

And what, monks, is right action?
Abstaining from killing living beings,
abstaining from taking what is not given,
abstaining from sexual misconduct.
This, monks, is called right action.

And what, monks, is right livelihood?
Here, monks, a noble disciple,
having given up wrong livelihood,
makes his living by right livelihood.
This, monks, is called right livelihood.

And what, monks, is right effort?
Here, monks, a monk,
for the non-arising of evil unwholesome states that have not arisen,
he arouses desire, makes an effort, stirs up energy,
exerts his mind and strives.
For the abandoning of evil unwholesome states that have arisen,
he arouses desire, makes an effort, stirs up energy,
exerts his mind and strives.
For the arising of wholesome states that have not arisen,
he arouses desire, makes an effort, stirs up energy,
exerts his mind and strives.
For the lasting and non-confusion of wholesome states that have arisen,
for their increase, abundance, development and fulfilment,
he arouses desire, makes an effort, stirs up energy,
exerts his mind and strives.
This, monks, is called right effort.

And what, monks, is right mindfulness?
Here, monks, a monk,
dwells contemplating the body in the body,
ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful,
having put away covetousness and grief for the world;
he dwells contemplating feelings in feelings,
ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful,
having put away covetousness and grief for the world;
he dwells contemplating mind in mind,
ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful,
having put away covetousness and grief for the world;
he dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas,
ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful,
having put away covetousness and grief for the world.
This, monks, is called right mindfulness.

And what, monks, is right concentration?
Here, monks, a monk,
quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unwholesome states,
with thought and examination, with rapture and happiness born of seclusion,
enters and dwells in the first jhana.
With the stilling of thought and examination,
with inner confidence and unification of mind,
without thought and examination, with rapture and happiness born of concentration,
he enters and dwells in the second jhana.
With the fading away of rapture, he dwells in equanimity,
mindful and clearly comprehending,
and experiences happiness with the body.
Of this the noble ones declare:
'He has equanimity, is mindful, and dwells happily';
he enters and dwells in that third jhana.
With the abandoning of happiness and the abandoning of suffering,
and with the earlier disappearance of joy and grief,
neither painful nor pleasant, with mindfulness purified by equanimity,
he enters and dwells in the fourth jhana.
This, monks, is called right concentration.

This, monks, is called
the noble truth of the way leading to the cessation of suffering.

Thus he dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas internally,
or he dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas externally,
or he dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas both internally and externally.
or he dwells contemplating the nature of arising in dhammas,
or he dwells contemplating the nature of passing away in dhammas,
or he dwells contemplating the nature of both arising and passing away in dhammas.

Or else, that 'there are dhammas'

He dwells independent,
and clings to nothing in the world.
