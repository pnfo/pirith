The Great Discourse on the Foundations of Mindfulness

Thus have I heard. At one time the Blessed One was living among the Kurus, at a market town of the Kurus named Kammasadamma. There the Blessed One addressed the monks: 'Monks.' 'Venerable sir,' those monks replied. The Blessed One said this:

Monks, this is the one-way path for the purification of beings, for overcoming sorrow and lamentation, for the ending of pain and grief, for attaining the right method, for the realisation of Nibbana: namely, the four foundations of mindfulness. What four? Here, monks, a monk dwells contemplating the body in the body, ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world;

he dwells contemplating feelings in feelings, ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world;

he dwells contemplating the mind in the mind, ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world;

he dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas, ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world.

The summary is finished.

And how, monks, does a monk dwell contemplating the body in the body? Here, monks, a monk who has gone to the forest, to the foot of a tree or to an empty hut sits down, folds his legs crosswise, holds his body straight and establishes mindfulness in front of him. Mindful he breathes in, mindful he breathes out. Breathing in long, he knows 'I breathe in long'; breathing out long, he knows 'I breathe out long'. Breathing in short, he knows 'I breathe in short'; breathing out short, he knows 'I breathe out short'. He trains: 'I shall breathe in experiencing the whole body'; he trains: 'I shall breathe out experiencing the whole body'. He trains: 'I shall breathe in calming the bodily formation'; he trains: 'I shall breathe out calming the bodily formation'.

Just as a skilled turner or his apprentice, monks, making a long turn knows 'I make a long turn', or making a short turn knows 'I make a short turn'; so too, monks, a monk breathing in long, he knows 'I breathe in long'; breathing out long, he knows 'I breathe out long'. Breathing in short, he knows 'I breathe in short'; breathing out short, he knows 'I breathe out short'. He trains: 'I shall breathe in experiencing the whole body'; he trains: 'I shall breathe out experiencing the whole body'. He trains: 'I shall breathe in calming the bodily formation'; he trains: 'I shall breathe out calming the bodily formation'.

Thus he dwells contemplating the body in the body internally, or he dwells contemplating the body in the body externally, or he dwells contemplating the body in the body both internally and externally.

He dwells contemplating the nature of arising in the body, or the nature of passing away in the body, or the nature of both arising and passing away in the body.

Or mindfulness that 'there is a body' is established in him, just to the extent needed for knowledge and awareness. He dwells independent, not clinging to anything in the world. In this way too, monks, a monk dwells contemplating the body in the body.

The section on in-and-out breathing is finished.

The section on the postures is finished.

In this way too, monks, a monk dwells contemplating the body in the body.

The section on clear comprehension is finished.

Again, monks, a monk reviews this very body, up from the soles of the feet and down from the top of the hair, bounded by skin and full of many kinds of impurity:

'In this body there are head hairs, body hairs, nails, teeth, skin, flesh, sinews, bones, bone marrow, kidneys, heart, liver, diaphragm, spleen, lungs, intestines, mesentery, contents of the stomach, faeces, bile, phlegm, pus, blood, sweat, fat, tears, grease, spittle, snot, oil of the joints and urine.'

In this way too, monks, a monk dwells contemplating the body in the body.

The section on attention to repulsiveness is finished.

In this way too, monks, a monk dwells contemplating the body in the body.

The section on attention to the elements is finished.

The first recitation section is finished.

Again, monks, a monk dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas in terms of the four noble truths. And how, monks, does a monk dwell contemplating dhammas in dhammas in terms of the four noble truths?

Here, monks, a monk knows as it really is: 'This is suffering'; he knows as it really is: 'This is the origin of suffering'; he knows as it really is: 'This is the cessation of suffering'; he knows as it really is: 'This is the way leading to the cessation of suffering'.

And what, monks, is the noble truth of suffering? Birth is suffering, ageing is suffering, death is suffering; sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief and despair are suffering; association with the disliked is suffering, separation from the liked is suffering, not getting what one wants is suffering; in brief, the five aggregates of clinging are suffering.

And what, monks, is birth? The birth of the various beings in the various orders of beings, their being born, descent, production, the appearance of the aggregates, the obtaining of the sense bases: this, monks, is called birth.

And what, monks, is ageing? The ageing of the various beings in the various orders of beings, their decay, broken teeth, grey hair, wrinkled skin, decline of life, ripening of the faculties: this, monks, is called ageing.

And what, monks, is death? The passing of the various beings from the various orders of beings, their passing away, breaking up, disappearance, dying, death, completion of time, the breaking up of the aggregates, the laying down of the body, the cutting off of the life faculty: this, monks, is called death.

And what, monks, is sorrow? The sorrow, sorrowing, sorrowfulness, inner sorrow and deep inner sorrow of one who has met with some misfortune or is touched by some painful state: this, monks, is called sorrow.

And what, monks, is lamentation? The wailing and lamenting, the act of wailing and lamenting, the state of wailing and lamenting of one who has met with some misfortune or is touched by some painful state: this, monks, is called lamentation.

And what, monks, is pain? Bodily pain, bodily discomfort, the painful and uncomfortable feeling born of bodily contact: this, monks, is called pain.

And what, monks, is grief? Mental pain, mental discomfort, the painful and uncomfortable feeling born of mental contact: this, monks, is called grief.

And what, monks, is despair? The trouble and despair, the state of being troubled and in despair of one who has met with some misfortune or is touched by some painful state: this, monks, is called despair.

And what, monks, is the suffering of association with the disliked? Here, whoever has unwanted, unloved, unpleasing forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangible objects and mental objects, or whoever has those who wish him harm, ill, discomfort and bondage: meeting, coming together, joining and mixing with them. This, monks, is called the suffering of association with the disliked.

And what, monks, is the suffering of separation from the liked? Here, whoever has wanted, loved, pleasing forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangible objects and mental objects, or whoever has those who wish him good, welfare, comfort and security, such as mother, father, brother, sister, elders, juniors, friends, companions or blood relatives: not meeting, not coming together, not joining and not mixing with them. This, monks, is called the suffering of separation from the liked.

And what, monks, is the suffering of not getting what one wants? In beings subject to birth, monks, this wish arises: 'Oh, that we were not subject to birth, that birth would not come to us!' But this cannot be gained by wishing. This too is the suffering of not getting what one wants.

In beings subject to ageing, monks, this wish arises: 'Oh, that we were not subject to ageing, that ageing would not come to us!' But this cannot be gained by wishing. This too is the suffering of not getting what one wants.

In beings subject to sickness, monks, this wish arises: 'Oh, that we were not subject to sickness, that sickness would not come to us!' But this cannot be gained by wishing. This too is the suffering of not getting what one wants.

In beings subject to pain, monks, this wish arises: 'Oh, that we were not subject to pain, that pain would not come to us!' But this cannot be gained by wishing. This too is the suffering of not getting what one wants.

In beings subject to despair, monks, this wish arises: 'Oh, that we were not subject to despair, that despair would not come to us!' But this cannot be gained by wishing. This too is the suffering of not getting what one wants.

And what, monks, in brief, are the five aggregates of clinging that are suffering? They are the aggregate of clinging to form, the aggregate of clinging to feeling, the aggregate of clinging to perception, the aggregate of clinging to formations and the aggregate of clinging to consciousness. These, monks, are called, in brief, the five aggregates of clinging that are suffering. This, monks, is called the noble truth of suffering.

And what, monks, is the noble truth of the origin of suffering? It is this craving that leads to renewed existence, accompanied by delight and lust, delighting now here, now there; namely, craving for sensual pleasures, craving for existence and craving for non-existence.

But where, monks, does this craving arise when it arises, and where does it settle when it settles? Whatever in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles. And what in the world is dear and pleasing? The eye in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles. The ear in the world ... the nose in the world ... the tongue in the world ... the body in the world ... the mind in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles.

Forms ... sounds ... smells ... tastes ... tangible objects ... mental objects in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles.

Eye-consciousness ... ear-consciousness ... nose-consciousness ... tongue-consciousness ... body-consciousness ... mind-consciousness in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles.

Eye-contact ... ear-contact ... nose-contact ... tongue-contact ... body-contact ... mind-contact in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles.

Feeling born of eye-contact ... feeling born of ear-contact ... feeling born of nose-contact ... feeling born of tongue-contact ... feeling born of body-contact ... feeling born of mind-contact in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles.

Perception of forms ... perception of sounds ... perception of smells ... perception of tastes ... perception of tangible objects ... perception of mental objects in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles.

Volition regarding forms ... volition regarding sounds ... volition regarding smells ... volition regarding tastes ... volition regarding tangible objects ... volition regarding mental objects in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles.

Craving for forms ... craving for sounds ... craving for smells ... craving for tastes ... craving for tangible objects ... craving for mental objects in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles.

Thought about forms ... thought about sounds ... thought about smells ... thought about tastes ... thought about tangible objects ... thought about mental objects in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles.

Examination of forms ... examination of sounds ... examination of smells ... examination of tastes ... examination of tangible objects ... examination of mental objects in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving arises when it arises, there it settles when it settles.

This, monks, is called the noble truth of the origin of suffering.

And what, monks, is the noble truth of the cessation of suffering? It is the remainderless fading away and cessation of that very craving, the giving up and relinquishing of it, freedom from it, non-reliance on it. But where, monks, is this craving abandoned when it is abandoned, and where does it cease when it ceases? Whatever in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving is abandoned when it is abandoned, there it ceases when it ceases.

And what in the world is dear and pleasing? The eye in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving is abandoned when it is abandoned, there it ceases when it ceases. The ear ... the nose ... the tongue ... the body ... the mind in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving is abandoned when it is abandoned, there it ceases when it ceases.

Forms ... sounds ... smells ... tastes ... tangible objects ... mental objects in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving is abandoned when it is abandoned, there it ceases when it ceases.

Eye-consciousness ... ear-consciousness ... nose-consciousness ... tongue-consciousness ... body-consciousness ... mind-consciousness in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving is abandoned when it is abandoned, there it ceases when it ceases.

Eye-contact ... ear-contact ... nose-contact ... tongue-contact ... body-contact ... mind-contact in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving is abandoned when it is abandoned, there it ceases when it ceases.

Examination of forms ... examination of sounds ... examination of smells ... examination of tastes ... examination of tangible objects ... examination of mental objects in the world is dear and pleasing; there this craving is abandoned when it is abandoned, there it ceases when it ceases.

This, monks, is called the noble truth of the cessation of suffering.

And what, monks, is the noble truth of the way leading to the cessation of suffering? It is this noble eightfold path; namely, right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration.

And what, monks, is right view? Knowledge of suffering, knowledge of the origin of suffering, knowledge of the cessation of suffering and knowledge of the way leading to the cessation of suffering. This, monks, is called right view.

And what, monks, is right intention? The intention of renunciation, the intention of non-ill will and the intention of harmlessness. This, monks, is called right intention.

And what, monks, is right speech? Abstaining from false speech, abstaining from divisive speech, abstaining from harsh speech and abstaining from idle chatter. This, monks, is called right speech.

And what, monks, is right action? Abstaining from killing living beings, abstaining from taking what is not given and abstaining from sexual misconduct. This, monks, is called right action.

And what, monks, is right livelihood? Here, monks, a noble disciple gives up wrong livelihood and makes his living by right livelihood. This, monks, is called right livelihood.

And what, monks, is right effort? Here, monks, a monk generates desire, makes an effort, arouses energy, applies his mind and strives for the non-arising of unarisen evil, unwholesome states. He generates desire, makes an effort, arouses energy, applies his mind and strives for the abandoning of arisen evil, unwholesome states. He generates desire, makes an effort, arouses energy, applies his mind and strives for the arising of unarisen wholesome states. He generates desire, makes an effort, arouses energy, applies his mind and strives for the continuance, non-confusion, increase, abundance, development and fulfilment of arisen wholesome states. This, monks, is called right effort.

And what, monks, is right mindfulness? Here, monks, a monk dwells contemplating the body in the body, ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world; he dwells contemplating feelings in feelings, ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world; he dwells contemplating the mind in the mind, ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world; he dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas, ardent, clearly comprehending and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world. This, monks, is called right mindfulness.

And what, monks, is right concentration? Here, monks, secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unwholesome states, a monk enters and dwells in the first jhana, which is accompanied by thought and examination, with rapture and happiness born of seclusion. With the stilling of thought and examination he enters and dwells in the second jhana, which has inner confidence and unification of mind, is without thought and examination, and has rapture and happiness born of concentration. With the fading away of rapture he dwells equanimous, mindful and clearly comprehending, and experiences happiness with the body; he enters and dwells in the third jhana, of which the noble ones declare: 'He is equanimous, mindful, one who dwells happily.' With the abandoning of pleasure and pain, and with the earlier passing away of joy and grief, he enters and dwells in the fourth jhana, which is neither painful nor pleasant and has purity of mindfulness due to equanimity. This, monks, is called right concentration.

This, monks, is called the noble truth of the way leading to the cessation of suffering.

Thus he dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas internally, or he dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas externally, or he dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas both internally and externally. He dwells contemplating the nature of arising in dhammas, or the nature of passing away in dhammas, or the nature of both arising and passing away in dhammas.

Or mindfulness that 'there are dhammas' is established in him, just to the extent needed for knowledge and awareness. He dwells independent, not clinging to anything in the world.

In this way too, monks, a monk dwells contemplating dhammas in dhammas in terms of the four noble truths.

'Monks, this is the one-way path for the purification of beings, for overcoming sorrow and lamentation, for the ending of pain and grief, for attaining the right method, for the realisation of Nibbana: namely, the four foundations of mindfulness.'
